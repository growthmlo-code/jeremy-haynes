# PART A — OFFERS

## A1. Core principle: build the offer for COLD traffic

- Optimize the offer around cold audiences because "cold audiences are where true scale exists." Anything that works cold has the highest odds of also working warm — but the reverse is NOT true. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- A warm-audience win does "not by any means validate that it's probable to work with cold traffic." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- If your offer sucks, you will fail to crack advertising on warm OR cold audiences, because it "isn't aligned to people that actually want to buy it." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Cold-traffic reality:** cold audiences "don't know who you are, they don't care about you at all." The only thing that breaks through and converts fast is whether the offer "very clearly and evidently align[s] to them." If it aligns, every stat is "remarkably better immediately." If it doesn't: worst show rate imaginable, confused/cold leads looking for education, awful close rates. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

> "The show rate stats represent the enthusiasm that your leads actually have." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

## A2. In-market vs. needs-convinced (the audience that dictates the offer stack)

- First decide: are you selling to people who **actively want** what you have (in-market) or people who **need persuading** (needs-convinced)? They are "completely different offer stacks." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- You can sell the same thing but **frame it differently** for one demographic vs. the other. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- This choice also "dictate[s] the type of funnel" you use to sell the offer. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Diagnostic — when a ceiling is actually an offer problem:** if your existing audience starts showing up to calls/webinars "colder," "less sold," "less in-market" (curious, seeking education, open but needing much more convincing), offer alignment "could actually be one of the first major issues to consider." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

> Case: a client went from ~$100K/mo to "a little over 1.6 to 1.7 million dollars a month," hit a ceiling (colder leads), tried everything — campaign structures, ad angles, messaging angles, funnel types, sales teams, marketers — "nothing was working until they made an offer change." Then "everything started to go up again." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Caution:** an offer pivot is "a very large, significant change." Pursue other available solutions FIRST before a massive offer change. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

## A3. The messaging foundation under every offer (two cliffs + the bridge)

Messaging = "two different cliffs" with "a clear gap in the middle":

1. **Current problems & circumstances** — where the buyer stands now (address FIRST).
2. **Desired outcomes** — where they want to end up.
3. **The bridge** — what you sell (the offer). The buyer only cares about the bridge AFTER you've successfully articulated their problems, circumstances, and desired outcomes. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

> "the most important thing to do with your offer is aligning the offer stack to solving the problems, to overcoming the negative circumstances somebody finds themselves in, and repositioning them for positive outcomes." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

## A4. How to build the offer stack (step by step)

**Step 1 — Write the umbrella NARRATIVE of your buyer.** The narrative "sits on top of all this stuff" and should make the problems, circumstances, and desired outcomes obvious. It comes from your ICP: "who is my ideal customer profile? Who are my people? What are they going through?" [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Step 2 — Derive the three variables from the narrative:**
- Problems (what they currently encounter / have failed at)
- Circumstances (the negative situations those problems create)
- Desired outcomes (what they actually want) [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Step 3 — Pick each item that "comes with it" to map directly onto one of those three variables.** Nothing in the stack is random; each variable "is picked strategically to appeal to a broader amount of people to buy that specific offer." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

> "when you pick what comes with it, the stuff that comes with it should be screamingly obvious based on you thinking through these things first." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Rule on the narrative:** "You never want to pull the narrative out of your ass." Get the narrative wrong (miss the problems/circumstances/outcomes, or only land a fragment) and "that in itself creates a bad offer." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

> "if you have an offer problem, you likely have an alignment problem to your market." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

### Worked examples of stack-to-narrative mapping (from the transcript)

- **Jeremy's Inner Circle** (min $100K/mo to join; avg member ~$300–400K/mo; 50+ members >$1M/mo; 8 who reached $5M/mo). Buyer narrative: wants to scale, has tried & been "screwed over by a bunch of marketing agencies," tried in-house and failed, now knows they "don't know enough" and seeks education from someone who speaks to their specific outcome (crack $1M/mo, then the next). Stack items chosen against that narrative: 35+ detailed how-to SOPs (ad/organic/funnel/team-building/hiring strategies); weekly Friday group call (11am EST, recorded into a vault); one-on-one calls (up to twice/month, 30 min) for people who failed with group/mastermind vehicles; 4 in-person masterminds (winners present "here's how I did it" → mastermind vault) to overcome isolation; active group chat with qualified peers; staff logins so clients can train their people too. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- **Athena (EA/VA service)** charges "$3,000 a month" — well above competitors — by aligning the offer to what buyers have suffered (bad, low-skill, poorly-trained VAs that created more work). Stack: pre-vetted EAs served up (no interviewing yourself), ongoing training, AI training, a swap-in temp EA with knowledge transferred when yours is on vacation/medical, plus a manager for the EA you don't have to manage. Same core offer ("put an EA in your business"), aligned differently → commands a premium. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

## A5. Offers are DYNAMIC — add to the stack from feedback

Most people treat the offer as "set in stone." Wrong — "An offer is ideally dynamic." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Listen on sales calls for two things** (treated as categorically different for augmenting the offer):
1. **Objections**
2. **Delays** (a delay is technically an objection, but handled separately) [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

**Method:** have AI analyze all sales-call recordings to get "a comprehensive list of what specifically is preventing people from purchasing." Many objections "can be turned into additional variables that are included in our initial offer stack." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

- Example (money objection): instead of mentioning financing in passing, build it INTO the stack — capital partners / liquidity, pay over time, partial upfront + finance the rest, or post-purchase funding access. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- Example (real estate / his $3.8M home purchase — ~$1M down, ~$100K closing, ~$1.1M out of pocket; +$1.5M planned renovation): a realtor should add construction loans, renovation loans, demolition help, a subcontractor network, landscapers, architects to the stack so the deal doesn't die on the "what happens after the purchase" problem. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

> "You can deal with the next set of problems before the current problem is done by adding it to the original offer where the current problem's be[ing] solved." [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

## A6. Offer LAYERS & fragmented feedback

- Feedback is "fragmented" — picture a pie chart of who has which objection/request. The pie reveals which items belong in the CORE stack vs. which stay **dynamic/contextual add-ons.** [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- Don't force every add-on into the core offer: "If I tried to sell every steak with black truffle butter, I wouldn't sell the people who just want a steak." Keep options available AFTER the initial commitment — at that point "the upsells just become core offer stack additions." (Mastro's steak analogy; stock-vs-"tit job" car analogy.) [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- Deals stall at the finish line when sales reps aren't equipped with the real-time additions that strengthen the offer: "some people will refuse to buy a Ferrari unless they get the tit job." Equip sales reps with set-aside offer additions to close in real time. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

## A7. Market research for the offer (AI-driven)

- "Market research has never been more available." Feed an SOP + the video's lessons into an AI tool (Claude / ChatGPT / Gemini) and task an agent to analyze your ideal market on Reddit, X, Instagram, TikTok — find the narrative, problems, circumstances, desired outcomes BEFORE creating the offer. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]
- Also "literally just have conversations with the people that you think you want to be selling to" — an undermined tactic. Let the congregated data reveal the obvious stack items. [How To Build An Offer That Converts Cold Traffic Like Crazy]

# PART B — HOW OFFERS CHANGE FROM $300K/MO → $1M/MO (the 8 offer variables)

> Companies that crack $1M–$2M/mo "are dynamic with the offers and the offer variables." Offer decisions are "never random. It's calculated." Below are the eight variables, in the order Haynes presents them (he notes they "start moving in sequences for why we do what in each order"). [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers: What Actually Changes]

**Master symptom of an offer problem at scale:** "high friction across the entire marketing and sales system" — "If every single statistic at once is suffering and seems very high friction… that could be an indication… that you've got an offer issue." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers: What Actually Changes]

### Variable 1 — TAM (Total Addressable Market)
- "How many people at a given time are actually open to what you are selling?" [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Chet Holmes' market layers (from *The Ultimate Selling Machine*): **in-market → needs-convinced → mass market → non-buyers.** Layers "get colder and more costly the further up the spectrum" and the probability of buying diminishes — "less and less profitable as you hit larger and larger scale." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- **Small TAM** (e.g., a 5,000-person TAM — HIPAA-compliance ecom software, filtered by headcount, funding, revenue thresholds, # of decision-makers): aggressive creative cycling, faster offer fatigue, smaller but **concentrated** feedback that can hit many prospects' perception at once. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- **Large TAM** ("grandfather rule" markets — sales, real estate, stocks/equities; e.g., "upwards of a hundred million people" in sales): saturated but more in-market buyers (most probable & most profitable to convert); can scale ONE funnel to serious revenue before needing other funnels. "typically larger markets are safer. That's why they're so large." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- TAM is first because it determines how hard/easy everything is, how much you can realistically charge, and lets you "project the reality very easily." Find TAM via research / AI agents. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

### Variable 2 — Positioning
- How you want to be perceived in the market, relative to who you're selling to. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Boutique example (Apollo IE, ~10 produced, est. ~$3–8M each): offer-stack items flow from "how would the person who buys that type of car want their buying experience to be?" → limited-edition badge, extended warranty beyond the 4–5 yr norm of Ferrari/Lambo/Rolls, flying a mechanic anywhere on Earth (with disclosed extra cost for remote/private travel), shipping the car to them to test drive. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- "You never want to have to explain status." Positioning is mainly branding, but "what they want also becomes a clear part of what you can include." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

### Variable 3 — Price & Unit Economics
- "price is also what determines perceptual value." Undercharge (because a competitor overcharges) and you're "perceived as the lesser quality, cost-effective solution," which can repel who you want to attract. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Price deliberately high to speak to the rich (watch examples: FPJourne ~$300K entry, up to ~$0.5–1M, ~couple hundred/yr; Rolex >1M/yr = "tool watch"/mass; Vacheron Constantin 222 ~$80K after tax / ~$70K used). "These specific prices are not random. They price them there because they are actively attempting to speak to somebody based on the price." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- **Unit economics = "how much it's actually going to take to deliver whatever it is that you're selling."** Price "can absolutely be determined by your actual hard cost that it takes to get the outcome" — but you can also "wildly inflate a price to help position" for a richer demographic. Think general population vs. rich (and the layers within rich). [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

### Variable 4 — Unique Mechanism
- "the specific way that you go about delivering what you do as an outcome or a result" — "a unique way to deliver an outcome that's different than everybody else's." Matters most in saturated, high-competition markets; it's usually what lets you raise price and be perceived as premium. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Pool-filtration ladder (traditional chemical → saltwater → oxygenated, ~$3,500 install): same job (clean pool), different mechanism = different price/premium perception. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Pinball examples: **Jersey Jack** = "include literally everything" (topper, shaker motor, art blades, accessory lighting, bigger screen all standard) vs. **Stern** = mass-market quality but upsells those parts (toppers ~$1–3K each); **Barrels of Fun** = only 4 machines, 3 in the pinside.com top 100, 2 in top 10, via a deeper game-design mechanic from the designer they hired. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- His own mechanism example — **Master Internet Marketing** ($7,000 one-time): pay once, re-trained live every year for the lifetime of the offer (annual update, ~70% of class changed this year, 2 full weeks redone; classes 3–5 hrs, live, Q&A). "As simple as it might seem, it makes a huge difference." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

### Variable 5 — Sales Team
- The offer must be "stronger than your sales team." A strong offer can have "monkeys on the phone" and still sell; a weak offer + weak team produces a "fucked up set of stats" even on webinars. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Team strength dictates offer design & funnel: killer teams can close instant-form / free-lead-magnet leads (→ simpler offer, can run call funnels/education calls); weak teams need webinars (do the selling for them, hand them layups) and a stronger offer loaded with guarantees/refund policies to de-risk. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

### Variable 6 — Fulfillment / Architecture
- Fulfillment determines price and unit economics (cost to deliver), and must be designed from the **worst-case scenario.** [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Cautionary case: two competitors each sold "pay us this amount and we'll fulfill you for an entire year." Both had cracked $1M/mo; one shut the front-end offer in February, one in August when "every single stat across the board just became high friction." One had set aside cash and could calmly fulfill the remaining year; the other hadn't — had to fire people (no front-end cash flow), faced fulfillment expenses + "huge refund liability." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- Rules: **set aside the cash to fulfill in a worst case**, and before you sell, ask: "would you be comfortable fulfilling… if anything went wrong?" "the worst case scenario is typically the driving decision factor." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

### Variable 7 — Upsell Architecture / Retention Strategy
- Design upsells & retention INTO the offer from the start (recurring = retention strategy; transactional = upsell architecture). "It's not something that you think of later." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- **Grandfathering as a retention tactic:** Inner Circle historically $1,500 → $2K → $3K → $4K → $5K → $6K → $7K → now $10K/mo; everyone keeps the rate they joined at AS LONG AS they stay; drop and return = newest rate. Same grandfathering on Jeremy AI ($1,000/mo). [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- **"Slot machine effect" retention** (ref: *Addiction by Design*, casino best practices): ship something meaningful every week (Jeremy AI feature weekly; Inner Circle new content weekly, random SOP "bombs," quarterly masterminds). [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- **Combo/upsell architecture** (McDonald's combo analogy): Master Internet Marketing ($7K) bundles 2 months of Jeremy AI, then $750/mo (vs. the standard $1,000/mo = $250/mo saving) — the only place to get a Jeremy AI discount, architected from the start. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

### Variable 8 — Owner Dependency
- Very common at "only a couple hundred K a month": owner does all sales calls and/or is deep in fulfillment. Being too deep in sales → "dog shit fulfillment"; a big sales month "capitulates and lowers the sales the next month." [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]
- You "will likely become a constraint," so engineer limits and eventually remove yourself. His example: Inner Circle capped at 250 members (physical mastermind space is finite; masterminds "shouldn't have a fuckload of people"), one-on-ones only Mon/Wed/Fri 1–5pm, and those calls double as Jeremy AI training material ("multi-stack fulfillment-oriented action"). Then he launched **Jeremy's Half Circle** (no one-on-ones, no in-person mastermind; watch live; ~half price) to "open up a whole new market" without his personal involvement. [$300K/Mo Vs $1M/Mo Offers]

# PART C — MARKETING MATH / UNIT ECONOMICS

> "If you don't understand math, you don't understand marketing… you sure as hell don't stand a chance to crack million-dollar months." The three required math disciplines: **financial modeling, bottleneck analysis, closer math.** [If You Don't Understand Math, You Don't Understand Marketing]

## C1. Financial modeling

**Why model first:** before spending a dollar, see what every stat must be in a WORST case for each stat and still "turn that dollar back into a dollar, if not more." Also see how much each stat "can do before your whole operation collapses and becomes unprofitable." Build a model specific to YOUR exact funnel. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**AOV rule (critical):** always model on **cash collected per sale**, not revenue and not contracted dollars — "Put how much money you actually collect per sale because that'll make a huge difference to the stats." [If You Don't Understand Math…]

### Call-funnel model (top → bottom)
Inputs: Ad spend → Cost per call → Show rate (1st call) → [2nd-call booked % and 2nd-call show % — set to 100% for a one-call close] → Close rate → AOV (cash collected/sale).

Worked example (one-call close):
```
Ad spend:            $20,000
Cost per call:       $450      → 44 total calls generated
Show rate:           60%       → ~27 calls taken
Close rate:          22%       → 6 deals
AOV (cash collected):$14,000   → 6 × $14,000 revenue
Gross ROAS:          ~4:1   ("put a dollar in and we get about $4 back")
Net ROAS:            ~2.5:1 ("net about $2.50 back for every dollar")
```
Then deduct line items: fixed costs, team costs, sales team commission → total costs → net profit → net ROAS. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**Stress test:** drop show rate 60% → 30% and ROAS roughly halves ("We cut our rorowaz in half… by cutting our show rate in half"). [If You Don't Understand Math…]

### Webinar model
Inputs: Ad spend → Cost per registrant → Show rate → [Booked-call % and booked-call show % — set to 100% for direct-to-checkout] → Close rate → Cash collected/sale.

Worked example (book-a-call CTA):
```
Ad spend:                $5,000
Cost per registration:   $20     → 250 opt-ins
Show rate:               25%     → 63 show
Booked-call rate:        75%     ("outrageous"/unrealistic; realistic ≈ 30%)
...
Cash collected per sale: $3,500  ("very low" for a book-a-call webinar)
Gross:                   $3.83 per $1 spend
```
Then subtract all costs for net ROAS. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**Model-vs-reality use (Kadisha case):** owner reported a 2:1 ROAS but her quoted stats, plugged into the model, implied an **8:1**. Lesson: if the model and reality don't match, the owner is "making decisions in a false world." Plug team-reported stats into the model to detect lying/manipulated stats or confirm reality. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

## C2. Data hierarchy (what to track — and what NOT to)

Don't model every tiny number. **Leading indicators at the top: ROAS and collected dollars per booked call.** Then drill into the stats that make up those; then the sub-stats that make up those (only when something's broken). "we don't want to create a model that's overly complex." [If You Don't Understand Math…]

- A **cost per call** is itself made of three sub-stats: **CPM, link click-through rate, page conversion rate.** [If You Don't Understand Math…]
- Page conversion rate (call funnel) = **total qualified booked calls ÷ total link clicks** (NOT clicks-who-applied). [If You Don't Understand Math…]
- Early-funnel stats influence later ones: great opt-ins/show/conversion but a terrible close can be an advertising/messaging/qualification problem (bad leads), not the sales team. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

## C3. Bottleneck analysis

**Concept:** one constraint anywhere in advertising→marketing→sales holds back enormous revenue (river choking to a trickle). Map each step of YOUR funnel, put a real stat on each, compare to benchmarks, find the **most contracted** stat, and isolate attention there. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**Call-funnel steps to measure:** CPM → link CTR → page conversion rate (qualified booked calls ÷ clicks) → show rate → close rate → AOV. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**Webinar steps to measure:** CPM → link CTR → opt-in rate → webinar show rate → (inside webinar) retention rate + booking rate (or direct-to-checkout rate) → call show rate → close rate → AOV. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**The "doubles game":** when multiple stats are bad, fix whichever is easiest to **double** (or, for CPM, easiest to **halve**). Example: CPMs fine but 0.5% link CTR → put all attention on making better ads. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**Exception to "fix the most contracted stat":** a terrible close rate with every other stat great may be a **lead-quality / messaging / qualification** problem up-funnel, not the closers — "certain stats can be contracted, but it might not be that stat specifically." [If You Don't Understand Math…]

| Link click-through rate | 2% (ideal) |
| Page conversion rate (qualified booked calls ÷ clicks) | 3–5% |
| Show rate | ~60% avg; 70% = where you want to be; 30% below avg; 20s = big problems |
| Close rate | 15% (low) to 30% (high); most closers low-to-mid 20s |
| AOV | "the higher the better" (determined by offer/pricing: PIFs vs. financing/payment plans) |
[If You Don't Understand Math…]

### Benchmark KPIs — WEBINAR (cold traffic)
| Stat | Benchmark (Haynes) |
|---|---|
| CPM | ~$30–$80 |
| Link click-through rate | 2% |
| Opt-in rate | 20–50% (traffic/messaging dependent; profitable seen as low as 10%) |
| Cost per registration | ~$20 = conservative/middle |
| Webinar show rate | 10–30%+; avg ~20; aim for 30%+ |
| Retention rate (start of webinar → start of pitch) | 80%+ |
| Booking rate (people present at pitch → book) | 30%+ ideal; 10% worst; 10–20% for those who rush the close |
| Webinar call show rate | 70%+ (below 70% = a problem) |
| Close rate (calls booked from webinar = "layup deals") | 35%+ minimum |
| AOV | never below $5,000 for a book-a-call webinar; higher = better |
[If You Don't Understand Math…]

**How to measure show rate correctly (concurrency, not total joins):** use the **highest number of attendees present concurrently**, not everyone who clicked to join. Example: 1,000 registrants, 400 clicked to join (looks like 40%), but 200 present at the same time = **real 20% show rate.** Accurate show rate is required to compute retention rate. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**Close duration note:** closers "should stay in the close for as long as they should, which should be about an hour… just in the close" to hit the 30% booking rate. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

## C4. Closer math (reverse-engineer the goal)

**Principle:** start at the END (revenue goal) and work backward. Cash collected is the goal. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

### Worked back-calc to $1,000,000/mo
```
Goal (cash collected):        $1,000,000
AOV:                          $10,000
→ Deals needed:               $1,000,000 ÷ $10,000        = 100 sales
Close rate:                   20%
→ Calls taken needed:         100 ÷ 0.20                  = 500 calls taken
Show rate:                    60%
→ Calls booked needed:        500 ÷ 0.60                  = 833 calls booked
Cost per call:                $200
→ Ad spend needed:            833 × $200                  = ~$166,600 spend
Implied gross ROAS:           ~6:1
```
(Round decimals up/down at .5.) Verified by plugging these back into the call-funnel financial model — spend $166,600, $200 CPC, 60% show, 20% close, $10K AOV → ~$1M at ~6x ROAS. "our financial model align with the reverse engineered closer math." [If You Don't Understand Math…]

### From booked calls → how many closers you need (capacity math)
```
Closer capacity:          8 calls booked per closer per day
(at 60% show → ~5 shows/day, leaving time for admin/CRM/follow-up/EOD reports)

Working days/month:       30 days − 8 weekend days (2/wk × 4 wks) = 22 days
Calls to book/day:        833 ÷ 22                 = ~38 calls/day
Closers needed:           38 ÷ 8                    = ~5 closers
```
Result: 5 closers, 38 calls booked/day across 22 days, with ~$166,600 spend held at $200/call → the $1M month. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

### Bonus — "align your reality" (gap %)
Divide current reality by the required target to get % of the way to goal:
- Current spend $20,000 ÷ required $166,600 = **12%** of the way there.
- Example current reality to close the gap on: cost per booked call $275 (target $200), show rate 52% (target 60%), close rate 17% (target 20%), AOV $8,000 (target $10,000). [If You Don't Understand Math…]

> "It gives you a measurable benchmark of your current reality against where you actually need to be." [If You Don't Understand Math…]

**Tie-together order Haynes prefers:** start with **closer math** (reverse-engineer the goal) → confirm with the **financial model** → then run **bottleneck analysis** on the live funnel to make CPM + link CTR + page conversion yield the required cost per booked call, hold show rate, close rate, and AOV. [If You Don't Understand Math…]

# PART D — THE RESPONSIBILITY MATRIX (mindset behind staying rich)

> NOTE: Video 046 ("If You Want To Keep Getting Richer, You Need To Understand This") is a **mindset** framework, NOT marketing-math. Included briefly for fidelity; it contains no formulas. [If You Want To Keep Getting Richer…]

**The cycle, done right (3 steps):** you get richer → responsibility increases → **take MORE risk (expansion-oriented action), not less** → get richer → repeat, "clean and efficient." [If You Want To Keep Getting Richer…]

**The cycle, done wrong:** get richer → responsibility increases → feel overwhelmed → take LESS risk (contraction-oriented action) → make less → fixed expenses stay → pressure builds → back against the wall → forced to take risk anyway (fire people, shed lifestyle). A "trap door" / Path 2 = real contraction + victim mindset spiraling down. [If You Want To Keep Getting Richer…]

**Risk often = simple reinvestment** into what already works (more ad spend, more content, more consistency). His example: IG 3→5→8 posts/day; YouTube 2→4 videos/wk + a clips channel ("Haynes feed") at ~$5K–$30K/mo; hired a technical CSM with a bonus tied to an internal statistic. [If You Want To Keep Getting Richer…]
